# Problem Framing

## Domain

Recreational runners often plan their runs around their own schedules and training goals. A runner might decide that they want to run a certain distance on a particular day, sometimes with a target pace, route, or general time in mind. When running with someone else, their plans need to line up enough for them to do the run together.

Some social runs are organized through formal run clubs, which schedule group runs in advance and have a set time, location, and sometimes distance or pace groups. However, many runs are much more casual. Someone may already be planning to run five miles after class or go for a long run on Saturday morning and would be happy to have a friend join them. Running with friends can make running more social and enjoyable while also providing motivation and accountability. These runs are usually planned individually rather than as organized events.

For these casual runs, runners often coordinate by texting friends directly or using group chats. Because each person plans their runs independently, friends may not know about each other's upcoming runs unless someone reaches out. If they do decide to run together, they may also need to coordinate details such as time, distance, pace, route, and meeting location. Unlike an organized group run, there may not initially be an organizer or even an event to join. The run only becomes a social plan once someone shares it and another person is interested in joining.

## Bad situations

### Missing opportunities to run together

Two friends both enjoy running together, but usually plan their weekly runs independently. One Saturday morning, they each decide to run about five miles along the Charles River at around the same time. Neither person thinks to text the other because it is just a normal run they were already planning to do, and reaching out to friends before every ordinary run would create its own extra effort. Later that day, they find out that they were running in the same area at almost the same time. Both would have preferred to run together, but they missed the opportunity simply because neither knew about the other's plan.

### Difficulty coordinating a shared run

A runner knows they want to run sometime Saturday morning and would be happy to have friends join, but their exact time and distance are flexible. They ask a group chat if anyone wants to come. One friend can run after 10 AM, another needs to finish by 10:30 AM, and everyone has their own availability and preferences. The runner has to compare everyone's responses and ask follow-up questions before settling on a time and distance that works. Even when everyone would like to run together, coordinating their plans can take enough effort that it is easier for each person to plan their run separately.

## Corroboration

Research suggests that running with other people can help runners stay consistent. A 2022 study of more than 800 recreational runners found that people who started running with more partners also tended to run more often each week ([Running Together: How Sports Partners Keep You Running](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35368415/)). Runners with more running partners were also more likely to show up for a race they had registered for. This suggests that running with friends can provide more than just a social benefit and may also help people stay motivated and consistent.

Recent data also shows growing interest in the social side of running. Strava's 2025 Year in Sport report, which used activity data from its platform along with a survey of more than 30,000 people, found that the number of running clubs created on Strava grew 3.5 times compared to the previous year ([Strava Year in Sport 2025](https://press.strava.com/articles/strava-releases-12th-annual-year-in-sport-trend-report-2025)). Club-organized events also increased 1.5 times. While this data focuses on organized groups rather than casual runs between friends, it shows that many runners are interested in connecting and exercising with other people.

I also spoke with five recreational runners about how they plan casual runs with friends. Four said they usually coordinate through direct messages or group chats, and three described situations where they found out afterward that a friend had been running nearby or around the same time. They said they would like to run with their friends more often, but coordinating schedules and plans takes extra effort, so it is often easiest for everyone to plan their runs separately. This supports the idea that the problem is not necessarily a lack of interest in running together, but the effort required to discover when plans line up and coordinate a shared run.

There is also evidence that coordinating social runs through general-purpose communication tools can be difficult. Heylo, a platform for organizing groups including run clubs, describes organizers having to manage events and communication across multiple tools ([Heylo for Run Clubs](https://www.heylo.com/running-club)). Run clubs featured on its site say they previously relied on apps such as WhatsApp and GroupMe, where group chats could become noisy and information was harder to keep organized. These examples come from Heylo's own marketing materials, so they are not independent evidence and mostly describe larger running groups rather than casual plans between friends. Still, they show examples of runners dealing with coordination problems similar to the ones described above.

Together, these sources suggest that running with others is valuable and that there is growing interest in social running. The interviews also provide more direct evidence that casual coordination between friends can be a problem: runners may want to run together but still plan separately because discovering overlapping plans and coordinating the details takes extra effort. The interview sample is small, and most existing research focuses on organized running groups, but the same problems appeared across both the interviews and examples from larger running communities. The problem is therefore not necessarily finding people who want to run socially, but making already planned runs visible early enough for friends to realize when their plans nearly match.

## Workarounds and comparables

### Workarounds

Runners often coordinate casual runs through direct messages or group chats. These are convenient because people already use them, and they work well when someone already knows that they want to organize a run with particular friends. However, they require someone to initiate the conversation. Reaching out to friends before every ordinary run creates extra effort, while not asking can mean never discovering that a friend's plans already lined up. Group chats therefore work better for coordinating a run once someone decides to organize one than for discovering casual opportunities that already exist.

For more complicated plans, runners can use calendars or scheduling tools to compare availability and keep the final details in one place. These tools are helpful when a group is intentionally organizing a run, but can feel like too much effort for something casual that someone was already planning to do.

### Comparables

#### Strava

Strava is a social fitness platform where runners can share completed activities and connect with other runners. Its activity-sharing features make it easy to discover that a friend ran nearby, but often after the opportunity to run together has already passed. Strava also supports planning future runs through Strava Clubs, where club owners and administrators can create organized group events.

This leaves a gap between the two: an individual runner's future intent. Someone who already plans to run five miles after class does not have the same lightweight way to make that plan visible to friends before the run happens without turning it into an organized club event.

#### Heylo

Heylo is designed for organizing communities such as run clubs. It brings events, RSVPs, routes, pace groups, attendance, and event conversations into one place, making it easier for organizers to manage recurring group activities.

Heylo demonstrates that many coordination problems can already be solved once a group and event exist. Its focus is managing established communities and their events, rather than exposing casual future plans between individual friends.

## Solution sketch

A potential solution is a social running app where people can share casual runs they are already planning with their friends. Runners could see their friends' upcoming runs in one place, along with basic details such as when and where they are running, their planned distance, and pace. If a friend's run works for them, they could simply join it. Making these plans visible ahead of time would help friends notice opportunities to run together without someone having to reach out every time.

A run could either have fixed details or include some flexibility. For example, someone might plan to run five miles at 10 AM but indicate that they would be willing to start anytime between 9:30 and 11 AM. If the current plan works for a friend, they could simply join it. If it almost works, the friend could suggest a specific change within the runner's flexibility, such as starting at 10:30 instead. The runner who created the plan could accept or reject the suggestion, allowing friends to coordinate small adjustments without turning the run into a formal scheduling process.

# Stakeholders

- **Runners sharing their plans:** Runners who are already planning to go for a run and would be open to having friends join them, but may not want to individually reach out every time they run.
- **Runners looking to join friends:** Runners who would like to run socially and may be interested in joining a friend's run when their schedules, distances, or other preferences line up.

The same person can take on either role depending on the run.
